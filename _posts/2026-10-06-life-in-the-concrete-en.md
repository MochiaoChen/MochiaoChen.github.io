## I. Where two etymologies meet

Begin with the words themselves.

An early occurrence of the Chinese word *juti*, now meaning “concrete,” appears in *Mencius*, in a description of three disciples of Confucius: they possessed the whole constitution of a sage, though on a smaller scale. Zhao Qi glossed the term as having all the limbs and members present. *Ju* means complete or fully equipped; *ti* refers to the body's parts. The original meaning was therefore **completeness**: every part is there, nothing missing.

The Latin *concretus* comes from *con-crescere*, to grow together: many things joining into a single entity. Its opposite, *abstractus*, comes from *abs-trahere*, to draw away or remove.

Two languages far apart arrive at the same judgement: **the concrete is completeness; abstraction is subtraction.**

Everyday usage reverses this relationship. “Concrete” suggests the small, the local, the technical: “Be more specific”; “That is a practical detail.” “Abstract” suggests height, depth, the whole picture. Abstraction seems to stand higher and see more; the concrete becomes the detail beneath it.

Hegel challenged this reversal. For him the abstract is impoverished: “being,” the most abstract concept, is also the emptiest, so empty that it is indistinguishable from “nothing.” The concrete is a rich accumulation of determinations. Marx made the point more explicit in his 1857 introduction to the critique of political economy: the concrete is concrete because it synthesises many determinations into a unity of the diverse. His example is “population.” It seems a concrete starting point, yet remains an empty abstraction unless we specify the classes that compose it and the relations of wage labour and capital on which those classes depend. Add these determinations back, layer by layer, and population becomes concrete again: a rich totality of determinations and relations.

This is where the essay begins. To read “life is in the concrete” as merely “pay attention to detail” or “keep your feet on the ground” is to make it shallow. Life takes place **where determinations are densest**, where many relationships grow together. Every abstraction removes something from that point in exchange for tractability. The exchange is often necessary and sometimes fatal. What follows is an accounting of that bargain.

## II. Abstraction is subtraction

We need abstraction. Without number there is no trade; without law, no cooperation among strangers; without prices, no economic system coordinating billions of people. Abstraction is a survival technology. It compresses unmanageable diversity into forms that can be calculated, communicated, and compared.

But compression has a price, and that price has a consistent structure: **what gets compressed away is precisely what makes something itself.**

Take the simplest example. “Three” can refer to three apples, three people, three heartbeats. It is universal because it discards the apples' sweetness, the people's faces, the rhythm of the heartbeats. It retains their one shared feature: they admit a one-to-one correspondence. Mathematics draws its power from this, and its indifference. It never goes wrong because it never touches the things that can go wrong.

Here is an observation to which we will return: **abstractions do not die.** The number three does not die; neither does the triangle or the concept of justice. Only concrete things die: this apple, this person, this heartbeat. Mortality and concreteness are two sides of the same thing. The more particular and irreplaceable something is, the more its disappearance is a real disappearance. An instance of an abstraction may break while the abstraction remains intact. When a concrete thing breaks, the world loses a combination that will never appear again.

“Life is in the concrete” therefore has another, harder meaning. Life is life because it ends. It ends because it is particular.

## III. Misplaced concreteness: when the map eats the territory

In *Science and the Modern World* (1925), Alfred North Whitehead named the “fallacy of misplaced concreteness”: taking what has been abstracted to be the most real thing, then using it to measure or even deny its source.

His target was the worldview of modern physics. Galileo and Descartes divided the world into primary qualities—extension, shape, motion, susceptible to mathematics—and secondary qualities—colour, smell, felt temperature, relegated to the subjective. The division was enormously successful as a method. Its consequence was the belief that the world was really nothing but moving material particles, while colour and fragrance were illusions added by the mind. Whitehead's irony was sharp: nature had become a dull affair, soundless, scentless, colourless, merely matter hurrying endlessly and meaninglessly about. Poets should have praised themselves, since their minds supplied the rose's fragrance.

Social science has a more practical version. James C. Scott's *Seeing Like a State* (1998) studies how modern states simplify societies to make them legible: planting forests in grids of a single species, imposing permanent surnames, organising cities into geometric plans. Each simplification is administratively reasonable. A state must count, tax, and conscript. The trouble starts when it believes that its simplified map is reality and uses power to remake reality in the map's image. Eighteenth-century German scientific forestry produced orderly Norway spruce plantations. The first generation yielded well; the second suffered forest decline. The shrubs, dead wood, insects, and fungi removed as disorder had been part of the soil's cycle.

Scott calls the displaced knowledge *mētis*: practical intelligence or cunning. An old sailor's knowledge of a bay, a farmer's knowledge of a field. Such knowledge **works only in particular situations and cannot be fully written into rules**.

Students of economics and finance should linger here. When Hayek defended markets against central planning in “The Use of Knowledge in Society” (1945), his central argument concerned particularity. The economic problem is not how to allocate given resources, because resources never exist as a centralised, given totality. Relevant knowledge is scattered among countless individuals, much of it knowledge of particular circumstances of time and place: which ship will return empty this week, whose stock of raw material is about to expire, which worker can repair an old machine. Aggregate this knowledge and its value is lost. Prices work because no one needs to aggregate it. Each person acts on local knowledge and a price signal.

Hayek's defence of markets is thus an epistemological argument about the concrete: **the most important knowledge cannot be abstracted, so a system dependent on abstract aggregation will go blind at crucial points.** Curiously, a price is itself an abstraction. A market mobilises the greatest amount of concrete knowledge with the least abstraction. It works by keeping abstraction thin.

## IV. Knowing more than we can say

Michael Polanyi opened *The Tacit Dimension* (1966) with a sentence much quoted since: “We can know more than we can tell.”

His example was recognising a face. You can recognise a familiar face among a thousand, yet cannot explain how. You cannot provide rules by which another person could recognise it without further help. Riding a bicycle, tasting wine, diagnosing a patient, judging a counterparty's reliability: all have the same structure. Knowledge resides in a grasp of a concrete whole. Break it into articulable components and it disappears.

Polanyi went further. Tacit knowing has a from-to structure: from details held in subsidiary awareness, we attend to a whole held in focal awareness. A pianist attends to the music while finger movements remain subsidiary. Turn attention to the fingers and the performance falls apart. **We grasp a concrete whole only when we are not taking it apart.**

Again this contradicts the ordinary idea of concreteness. We think it means drilling down into detail. Polanyi means indwelling the details and looking through them towards the whole. Inhabiting detail and staring at detail are different relationships. One produces understanding; the other, paralysis.

This has a new resonance in the age of language models. Their abilities emerge from statistical regularities. What they learn from enormous text corpora is knowledge that can be expressed, plus some tacit structures inferred from what has been expressed. They show broad competence across many kinds of articulated knowledge. Faced with a situation no one has ever written down, they need to be told about it, much like a newcomer. The stronger the model, the scarcer a person's unwritten, concrete knowledge appears. We will return to this.

## V. Ivan Ilyich's syllogism

A passage in Tolstoy's *The Death of Ivan Ilyich* (1886) may be literature's most precise rendering of the distance between the abstract and the concrete.

Ivan Ilyich learnt a logical example at school: Caius is a man; men are mortal; therefore Caius is mortal. All his life he accepted it as correct, but only for Caius, never himself. Caius was a man in general, an abstract man; dying was reasonable for him. Ivan had never been abstract. He was little Vanya, with his mother, father, Mitya and Volodya, his toys, coachman, and nurse. He remembered the smell of a striped ball and the rustle of his mother's silk dress as he kissed her hand. What did Caius know about the smell of that ball? Had Caius protested about the food at law school?

The passage's force lies in its refusal to refute the syllogism. It is correct. Ivan Ilyich will die; he is dying. Tolstoy shows something else: **a logically correct proposition may have no connection to a person at the level of existence.** “Men are mortal” includes Ivan Ilyich but does not touch him. It touches the abstract class “man,” a class he has never inhabited. He inhabits the smell of the striped ball.

Heidegger later supplied a term for this: Dasein's existence is always mine, *Jemeinigkeit*. Death cannot be delegated; no one can die in your place. Authentic being-towards-death therefore requires leaving the neutral, anonymous “one dies” behind and returning to “I will die.”

Tolstoy is harsher than Heidegger, and gentler. Ivan Ilyich's release at the end of the novella turns on a particular moment: his young son takes his hand, kisses it, and cries. He sees his son and his wife's tears, and feels sorry for them. Pain remains, but the fear of death vanishes. Salvation comes from no abstract insight. It comes from a kissed hand.

## VI. Statistical lives and lives with names

In 1968 Thomas Schelling published an essay with an arresting title: “The Life You Save May Be Your Own.” He distinguished identified lives from statistical lives.

A particular little girl falls into a well and a nation donates millions to rescue her. Spend those same millions on better road lighting and perhaps five deaths would be prevented over a decade. But those five people have no names or faces; no one knows who they are, not even themselves. Society is willing to spend far more on the former.

From a utilitarian perspective this is an irrational bias, later called the identifiable victim effect. Small, Loewenstein, and Slovic (2007) conducted an illuminating experiment: people shown a photograph and story of Rokia, a seven-year-old girl in Mali, donated more on average than those shown statistics about famine affecting millions of African children. The third group was more troubling. Given Rokia's story **and** the statistics, they donated less. Numbers did not increase sympathy; they diluted sympathy already awakened by a face.

Economics usually treats this as a cognitive defect to correct. Effective altruism proceeds along similar lines: overcome your preference for particular faces and allocate goodwill by statistical efficiency. This is a strong argument about resource allocation, and I do not intend to refute it.

Yet there is a structural paradox: **the engine of moral concern is concrete; its accounting is abstract.** Without responding to particular faces, people might never feel an impulse to help. Once abstract efficiency enters the calculation, the source of that impulse is labelled a bias. Abstract ethics feeds on concrete feeling while repeatedly declaring its host irrational.

In *The Brothers Karamazov*, Dostoevsky has Father Zosima tell the story of a doctor who loves humanity more and particular people less. In his dreams the doctor longs to sacrifice himself for mankind, but cannot endure sharing a room with anyone for two days. Another person's individuality presses upon his pride and limits his freedom. The more he hates individuals, the more fervently he loves humanity.

It is a precise pathology of abstract love. Loving humanity is easy: humanity does not smack its lips at dinner, interrupt you, or disappoint you. Abstract love is easy because its object offers no resistance. Particular people do. Love, in one sense, is the bearing of that resistance.

In 1936, little more than a month before his death, Lu Xun wrote a short essay, “This Too Is Life…” One night during his illness he asked Xu Guangping to turn on the lamp so that he could look around. He looked at familiar walls, their edges, the books, an unbound collection of prints, and felt that he was living. He wrote that boundless distances and countless people were connected to him. The line is often quoted as a grand expression of concern for the nation and humanity. Read in context, it starts from utterly ordinary furniture. The way to countless people begins at the edge of a wall. The order cannot be reversed.

## VII. Funes's curse: the concrete can also be death

The argument now seems secure: abstraction subtracts, the concrete is complete, and life belongs to completeness. Stop here, however, and the essay commits its own criticised mistake: making an abstract principle—“be concrete”—the destination.

Borges's “Funes the Memorious” (1942) tells of a young Uruguayan who gains perfect memory and perception after falling from a horse. He remembers every cloud's shape at every moment, every vein in every leaf. He can reconstruct an entire day, but doing so takes an entire day.

Funes struggles to understand why the class name “dog” includes individuals of different sizes and shapes. Worse, the dog seen from the side at 3:14 and from the front at 3:15 has the same name.

Borges doubts that Funes can really think: thinking requires forgetting differences, generalising, abstracting. Funes dies at nineteen of pulmonary congestion. He has drowned in the concrete.

The story reveals its other side: **pure concreteness is as unlivable as pure abstraction.** A mind with only the concrete cannot connect today to yesterday, learn from experience, or use language, since every word is already an abstraction. Funes's world has no gaps, and therefore no freedom.

Neuroscience offers a related observation. Human memory is physiologically lossy. Forgetting is an active, energy-consuming process; hippocampal neurogenesis has even been proposed as a mechanism that clears old traces. Evolutionarily this makes sense: an organism must extract patterns, and patterns appear only when details are discarded.

Life must therefore abstract. Does “life is in the concrete” still hold?

It does, but needs a more precise formulation. Return to Hegel and Marx. Their concrete was never mere sensory immediacy—this leaf, here and now. Hegel treated that immediacy as impoverished; the opening chapter of *Phenomenology of Spirit* argues that sense-certainty is the emptiest knowledge. Their concrete is what we return to after abstraction: a whole enriched by many determinations. Marx describes thought as ascending from the abstract to the concrete. “Ascending” is exactly right.

We now have two kinds of concreteness.

**The first concrete** is the concrete of the starting point: unexamined direct experience, rich and confused. Funes is trapped there.

**The second concrete** is the concrete of return: after abstraction has refined your understanding, you return to the thing itself and see more than before, more clearly.

Life belongs to the second. Abstraction is a necessary journey, but a round trip. There are two dead ends. Funes stays at the beginning. Those who leave and never return are the doctor who loves mankind and hates his neighbour, the forester who plants orderly spruce, and Ivan Ilyich, who believes men are mortal without ever thinking of himself.

## VIII. Cook Ding's eyes

Perhaps the finest Chinese image of the second concrete is Cook Ding carving an ox in the *Zhuangzi*, “Nourishing Life.”

He tells Lord Wenhui how his skill developed. At first he saw nothing but the whole ox. After three years he no longer saw it whole. Now he meets it through spirit rather than sight: sensory awareness stops and spirit moves, following its natural structure, entering gaps, passing through cavities, adapting to what is already there.

These stages match our framework.

First, the whole ox: a confused totality, rich but impossible to approach. This is the first concrete.

Second, the ox comes apart into sinews, joints, and textures. This is analysis and abstraction, dividing the whole into components. Many people mistake this for mastery.

Third, Cook Ding no longer looks part by part; he meets the ox's structure directly. Notice what he does: follows its own grain and what it is already like. His blade remains as sharp after nineteen years as if newly ground, because it fights nothing. It travels through existing spaces.

This is an image of the second concrete. His understanding is highly structured, but the structure never leaves this particular ox. Every ox differs. At a difficult tangle he still grows cautious, slows down, and attends completely. The greater his skill, the deeper his respect for the particular.

Contrast another Chinese story. The young Wang Yangming accepted Zhu Xi's investigation of things and believed principles lay within objects. He and a friend stared at bamboo for seven days, trying to investigate its principle, until he fell ill. Later, in Longchang, he came to the insight that mind itself is principle.

The story is usually read as the school of mind defeating the school of principle. Here it means something else: **staring at a concrete object does not mean entering the concrete.** Wang had no practical relationship with the bamboo. He did not grow it, cut it, or use it. It was merely an object of inspection. A gaze without a relationship produces fatigue, not understanding—Funes's predicament again. Cook Ding carves oxen daily. His relationship with them unfolds through labour and interaction. Concreteness emerges within relationships; it requires an exchange between you and the object.

We return to *concretus*: growing together. Concreteness is relational. The deeper and more numerous your encounters with something, the more concrete it becomes for you. A stranger is abstract. Eat dozens of meals with that person, quarrel a few times, see them staring into space in a hospital corridor, and the same person becomes concrete. They have not changed. What has changed is what has grown between you.

## IX. In an age of cheap abstraction

Now we can speak of the present.

For most of human history abstraction was scarce. Literacy, mathematical training, and systematic knowledge required years of education. Those who mastered abstraction held power. Ascending from the concrete to the abstract was therefore progress, and education taught people to generalise, model, and theorise.

That structure is changing. Language models make a particular kind of abstraction almost free: summarising, generalising, producing what is “generally speaking” the case. Ask a question and a clear, comprehensive, statistically plausible answer appears immediately.

Such answers share a quality: they are **average**. They arise from countless overlapping texts and tend towards the centre of the distribution. Career choices, they say, should consider interests, abilities, and market prospects. Relationships require communication and understanding. These statements are correct, like “men are mortal.” They include you without touching you.

When abstraction becomes cheap, the concrete becomes scarce: the situation only you know, the failure only you experienced, the understanding that grew between you and someone else, the detail you noticed after three years in an industry that appears in no report. Hayek's knowledge of particular times and places, Polanyi's unspoken knowledge, Scott's *mētis*: their value increases amid an excess of abstraction.

The practical implication is straightforward. Train yourself only in abstract work—organising information, applying frameworks, writing well-structured reports—and you compete with an ever-cheaper capability. What is hard to replace is the second concrete: after abstract training in a field, rooting yourself again in particular people and events and accumulating judgement available only on site.

There is a quieter danger. As people understand the world through increasingly averaged texts, their descriptions of their own lives may also converge towards the average. They recount experiences in generic vocabulary, understand emotions through templates, and eventually describe even suffering like everyone else. Abstraction then swallows not only their perception of the world but their perception of themselves. Ivan Ilyich's tragedy was thinking himself Caius almost until death. Someone raised in average language may become Caius earlier, more smoothly.

## X. The concrete as a verb

Finally, the concrete can become a verb: to make concrete, restoring abstractions to the determinations and relationships from which they came. It is a skill one can practise. Four directions may bring this essay to a close.

**Return nouns to actions.** Big words such as success, happiness, and meaning contain almost nothing on their own. Ask “while doing what, exactly?” and they acquire weight. Wanting freedom often means wanting mornings without an alarm or meetings without obligatory nodding. The latter is more real and guides action better.

**Return groups to a person.** Users, markets, young people, the Chinese: all useful abstractions. After using one, recall a particular, named person you actually know. If the abstraction fails for that person, the abstraction is probably at fault.

**Return the future to the next step.** Long-term plans are abstract. Life takes place only in the next step. However skilled Cook Ding becomes, each cut can enter only the joint before him.

**Treat attention as ethics.** In *The Sovereignty of Good* (1970), Iris Murdoch, drawing on Simone Weil, places moral life's centre before the moment of choice, in sustained attention. A mother-in-law initially thinks her son's wife vulgar, immature, and unworthy. She remains polite and conceals her feelings, but decides to look again and overcome her prejudice. Slowly the woman she sees changes: vulgarity becomes spontaneity, immaturity becomes liveliness. Outwardly nothing happens; Murdoch nevertheless sees a real moral event. In Weil's account, attention is generosity at its rarest and purest. Giving a particular person attention without preconception is, on this view, love's most basic form.

All four directions cost something. Abstraction saves effort by allowing you not to look. The concrete requires you to look, keep looking, and accept that what you see may contradict what you expected.

Return to *Mencius*: complete, though small. This may be the most honest description of an individual life. None of us commands the scale of a sage. Our lives are necessarily small: one city, one job, a few friends, unfinished plans. Yet smallness does not prevent completeness. On a small scale all the limbs can still be present, all the determinations accounted for, many relationships growing into something whole, belonging only to this one occasion, destined to end.

Life is in the concrete. The sentence finally reminds us of something simple: the only life you can really live is this one, this day, this person, the warmth of this bowl of rice. Everything else is talk about living.
