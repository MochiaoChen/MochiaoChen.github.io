*Human Life after AGI: An Inventory, Critique, and Projection from the History of Ideas*

------

## Introduction: The Shape of the Question

“What kind of life will humans lead after AGI?” is asked far more often than it is seriously examined. Public discussion usually offers two inadequate answers. Technological optimism promises that we will finally pursue art, philosophy, and love. Dystopian warnings imagine superfluous people, supported but idle. Both treat “no longer needing to work” as a self-explanatory condition, without asking how that condition is structured.

To ask the question properly, we must separate three problems, increasing in difficulty and requiring quite different responses.

**The first is distribution.** When labor ceases to be the principal input into value creation, who owns the output? Who decides what AGI's computing power is used for? This is political economy. We already have workable policy templates: progressive capital taxation, public ownership, sovereign wealth funds, universal dividends, and subsidies to labor's share. The difficulty lies in political feasibility and power, rather than a shortage of knowledge. We know possible ways forward; the question is whether anyone can make them happen.

**The second is purpose.** Without employment, what organizes time? Where does identity come from? What sustains a community? Sociology and psychology supply partial evidence from unemployment, retirement, leisure classes, and basic-income trials. Its applicability is limited: an entire society has never entered this condition simultaneously.

**The third is meaning.** When machines do almost everything better, faster, and more reliably, do human activities still have objective value? If a better machine-written poem, proof, or diagnosis is always available, what reason remains for me to produce one? This is a question for metaphysics and ethics. It has neither an engineering solution nor a policy solution. There may be a conceptual rearrangement—or a failure.

These problems are not simply parallel. **Distribution comes first in time**, because it determines the social form in which the other questions arise. **Meaning comes first in difficulty**, because redistribution cannot solve it. <u>An exceptionally fair world could still be a world in which everyone feels superfluous.</u>

Historical philosophers almost never discussed AGI. We must acknowledge that. Yet they repeatedly examined purpose and meaning, because **a life without compulsory labor has long existed for some people**. Classical citizens gained leisure through slavery; European rentiers and gentlemen through land and capital; retirees and heirs through insurance and accumulated wealth. Their lives, psychological outcomes, and cultural achievements are our only natural experiments in what happens after necessity.

AGI adds two genuinely new variables. It could extend this condition from a minority to everyone, removing leisure's relative status meaning. More fundamentally, it introduces the prospect of humans being comprehensively surpassed in ability. Historical leisure classes did not have to work, but their poetry, science, and political judgment could still be the best available. That would no longer be true.

This essay does not predict the future. Intellectual history cannot derive the social structure of 2050 from Plato. It can supply conceptual tools: turn an emotionally charged question into propositions with arguments, objections, and directions for empirical inquiry. The inventory broadly follows chronology, while considering each argument's present relevance and limits.

## Part I. Prehistory: Imagining Freedom from Labor

### 1. Aristotle's Automatic Shuttles

One of the earliest and most striking passages about automation appears in Aristotle's defense of slavery in *Politics* I (1253b33–1254a1). If tools could carry out their tasks on command, or anticipate them—like Daedalus's moving statues or Hephaestus's self-moving tripods—if shuttles wove and plectrums played by themselves, craftsmen would need no assistants and masters no slaves.

Aristotle offers this counterfactual to dismiss it. He regards it as impossible, preserving slavery's supposedly necessary place in household management. Yet its argumentative structure matters more than its conclusion: <mark><strong>automatic machinery and enslavement are functional substitutes.</strong></mark> To provide leisure for some, a society must have other people work for them or machines do so. These are branches of the same logic.

Marx quotes the passage in *Capital* I to mock economists' optimism about machinery's emancipatory effects. Norbert Wiener returns to it in *The Human Use of Human Beings* (1950), drawing a sharper inference: automated machines are the economic equivalent of slave labor, and human labor competing with them will be pushed toward its economic conditions. He does not mean workers literally become slaves. Under a purely market logic, their price is constrained by machinery's marginal cost, which approaches zero. Speculative in 1950, the argument can now be partially tested.

### 2. Scholē: Leisure as the Original Condition

Greek vocabulary contains a deeper insight. Leisure is σχολή (*scholē*), which became Latin *schola* and English “school”: a place of leisure. Work or busyness is ἀσχολία (*ascholia*), literally an absence of leisure.

The word formation embodies a philosophical orientation opposite to ours. Leisure is primary; work interrupts it. Modern language reverses this: work is primary, leisure occupies off-hours and the intervals between jobs. We define rest through work; the Greeks defined work through leisure.

Josef Pieper makes this reversal central to *Leisure: The Basis of Culture* (*Muße und Kult*, 1948). A civilization conceived as a world of total work has lost even the language for understanding leisure. It can only regard leisure as recovery for better work: leisure colonized by work. **In imagining life without work, we may be using concepts that already presume work's centrality, and that cannot adequately describe the life we want to imagine.**

### 3. Contemplation and Political Life

Aristotle offers two partly incompatible accounts of what leisure is for.

In *Politics* VII–VIII, he emphasizes education and citizenship. **Leisure has its own virtues, and is harder than work.** Sparta excels under the pressures of war and necessity but deteriorates in peace, because its education trains people for difficulties rather than their absence (1334a). Its empire destroys it because it cannot use leisure. Music belongs in a free person's education precisely because its purpose is not utility: it cultivates worthwhile activity in leisure (1337b–1338a).

In *Nicomachean Ethics* X, the highest happiness is θεωρία, contemplation: relatively self-sufficient, enduring, pursued for itself, and close to divine activity. This withdraws the good life from public affairs into the individual mind.

AGI sharpens the tension. **Contemplation is directly exposed to machines' superior abilities; civic action is relatively insulated.** If the best life consists in understanding the world and machines understand better, that route faces a challenge. If it consists in jointly deciding how we live, its subject cannot be outsourced. Arendt's choice of the second route in 1958 will matter later.

### 4. Cockaigne: Popular Abundance

Outside philosophy lies the medieval European land of Cockaigne: houses made of food, cooked geese walking to diners, rivers of wine, work forbidden, sleeping rewarded. Ballads, carnival traditions, and paintings repeatedly depict it; Bruegel's 1567 painting is the best-known example.

Its content is **pure consumption**. There is no contemplation, politics, creation, or difficulty. Because it directly negates deprivation, deprivation determines its imagination: the opposite of hunger is endless eating; the opposite of exhaustion is endless sleep.

This matters when assessing the default trajectory of abundance. **Popular, spontaneous visions of plenty tend toward unlimited consumption rather than contemplation.** Nietzsche's last man, Huxley's *Brave New World*, and Kojève's posthistorical animality are modern restatements of this pattern.

Elite utopias differ. More's *Utopia* (1516) assigns six hours of daily labor and the rest to free intellectual activity, with voluntary early-morning lectures. Bacon's *New Atlantis* (1627) makes technological expansion a social purpose: understanding causes and enlarging human capability. More concerns liberation of time; Bacon concerns expansion of power. Today's AGI discussion inherits Bacon's framework but encounters More's problem.

### 5. Zhuangzi's Mechanical Mind

An underappreciated passage in the *Zhuangzi*, “Heaven and Earth,” describes Zigong meeting an old gardener near Hanyin. The man laboriously carries water from a well in a jar. Zigong recommends a well-sweep, which accomplishes more with less effort. The gardener refuses: machinery brings mechanical affairs, which bring a mechanical mind; that mind disrupts simplicity and spiritual steadiness. He knows the device but is ashamed to use it.

The argument differs from the usual Western critique of technological consequences—unemployment, alienation, danger, or concealed being. It is constitutive: **using labor-saving machinery itself cultivates a structure of mind incompatible with a better mode of existence**. The issue is not what machines subsequently cause, but what a machine-dependent person has already become.

If sound, this is a devastating challenge to AGI. Even perfectly aligned, safe, fairly distributed AGI could change us through the simple habit of consulting it about everything. Research on cognitive offloading, including Sparrow and colleagues' 2011 study of Google's effects on memory and later work on GPS use and spatial memory, lends limited empirical support, although effect sizes and reproducibility remain disputed.

The text does not straightforwardly endorse refusal. Zigong is humbled, but Confucius later characterizes the gardener as understanding one side and missing the other, cultivating the inner while neglecting the outer. Pure rejection remains an attachment. The text's position is more complex than a simple denunciation of technology.

## Part II. How Labor Became the Human Essence

The anxiety surrounding non-work requires an intervening history. **The conviction that work constitutes our essence, meaning, and dignity is not an anthropological constant. It is a historically constructed apparatus roughly five centuries old.**

That means both that it can be dismantled and that dismantling it will hurt: it permeates identity, intimate relationships, citizenship, and political legitimacy.

### 1. Two Faces of the Hebrew Tradition

In Genesis 2:15, humans tend and keep Eden before the Fall: labor is an entrusted responsibility within creation. In 3:17–19, cursed ground and bread earned by sweat make labor punishment and necessity. Work is both dignity and curse.

Medieval practice layered these meanings. Benedictine *ora et labora*, prayer and labor, gives physical work spiritual value against *acedia*: more than laziness, an aversion to becoming what one's soul ought to be. Yet contemplation remained superior in the hierarchy of activities; Aquinas follows Aristotle's ordering in the *Summa Theologiae*.

### 2. The Reformation's Decisive Leap

Luther extends *Beruf*, a calling, to secular occupations. A farmer's labor and a monk's prayer possess equal value before God, removing contemplation's privileged position.

Calvinism adds another mechanism. Conduct cannot earn salvation under predestination, but worldly success can be read as evidence of election. Weber's *The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism* (1904–1905) argues that systematic, ascetic, potentially limitless work becomes an inner need. Eventually detached from theology, it encloses people in an “iron cage”—more literally, a shell hard as steel.

The historical causal claim is highly contested. Becker and Woessmann's 2009 study of Prussian counties, for instance, proposes literacy rather than work ethic as a mechanism connecting Protestantism and prosperity. For this essay, the useful point is descriptive: <mark><strong>modern people's psychological attachment to labor has a historical origin, and its original motives have become detached from those sustaining it today.</strong></mark> That is what the cage metaphor captures.

### 3. Labor Enters Philosophy's Core

Between the seventeenth and nineteenth centuries, labor rises from an economic to a metaphysical category through three decisive moments.

**Locke (1689)** grounds property in labor in chapter V of the *Second Treatise*. We own our bodies and therefore our labor; mixing it with something held in common establishes ownership. Labor becomes a moral foundation of property and, implicitly, civic standing. The difficulty of granting full moral standing to non-workers persists in disputes over welfare dependency.

**Hegel (1807)** gives labor a stronger role in the master–slave dialectic. The master consumes objects through the slave and depends on recognition from someone he refuses to recognize as an equal. The slave transforms objects through restrained desire, seeing consciousness externalized in them and acquiring an independent self-relation. Labor forms the subject.

If subjectivity depends on shaping things and delaying gratification, a world of instant satisfaction and machine-performed transformation could obstruct its formation. It need not make people unhappy; it could leave them in the master's hollow dependence on unequal recognition. AGI occupies the slave's position, and Hegel's dialectic tells us that the slave ultimately prevails.

**Marx (1844)** defines productive activity as human species-being in the *Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts*. Humans can produce freely, consciously, and according to beauty, making nature their inorganic body. Alienation from the product, productive activity, species-being, and one another thus means losing our essence, rather than merely receiving an unfair share.

### 4. The Political Function of the Work Ethic

Bauman's *Work, Consumerism and the New Poor* (1998) adds a political dimension. Early industrial work ethics disciplined people accustomed to seasonal, need-based labor and “Saint Monday” into factory routines. E. P. Thompson's 1967 study documents clocks, fines, and moral education in punctuality.

In postindustrial society, Bauman argues, the ethic increasingly **legitimates inequality** by presenting unemployment as moral failure rather than mobilizing production.

It will not automatically disappear when human labor loses value. **A rule that non-workers deserve nothing could continue allocating resources after its economic premise has vanished.** This is a dangerous transition risk precisely because the moral intuition is already installed and requires no fresh justification.

## Part III. Two Remarkably Precise Rehearsals

Two texts, written in 1858 and 1930 without computers, approach today's problem with unsettling precision.

### 1. Marx's Fragment on Machines

#### The Text

The *Grundrisse*, written in 1857–1858, first published in Moscow in 1939 and more widely received after its 1953 republication, contains the pages later called the “Fragment on Machines.” Its framework would change substantially in *Capital*, but it contains Marx's most radical extrapolation of automation.

First, workers cease to apply tools directly to objects and instead become conscious links in an automated system, standing alongside production as supervisors and regulators.

Second, the system embodies accumulated social science and technical knowledge rather than individual skill. Marx calls this the **general intellect**: knowledge becomes a direct productive force, materialized in fixed capital.

Third, capitalism measures value through labor time while technological development eliminates necessary labor time. As direct labor's role approaches zero, production founded on exchange value undermines itself.

Fourth, wealth's measure shifts from labor time to **disposable time**: time for everyone's full individual development, which in turn becomes a productive force.

#### The More Restrained Account in Capital III

Chapter 48 distinguishes the realm of necessity from the realm of freedom. Material production always entails interchange with nature; society can rationalize it and minimize its expenditure of effort, but cannot abolish it. Freedom begins beyond this realm while depending on it. Shortening the working day is the fundamental condition.

<u>The later Marx promises to compress necessity, not abolish it.</u> AGI optimism often adopts the *Grundrisse*'s expansive register, while a more robust judgment resembles *Capital* III: necessity can become very small, but its amount and distribution remain political questions.

#### Inheritance and Criticism

Italian *operaismo* and post-workerism revived the fragment after the 1960s. Virno, Negri and Hardt, and Lazzarato interpret knowledge, affect, communication, and cooperation as socially produced sources of value that cannot be entirely privatized. These arguments feed into accelerationism and Bastani's *Fully Automated Luxury Communism* (2019).

Benanav's *Automation and the Future of Work* (2020) presents a serious empirical objection. **Evidence for technological unemployment has generally been weaker than its theoretical predictions.** If automation drove deteriorating labor markets, productivity growth should accelerate; instead it has slowed across major advanced economies since the 1970s. Solow's productivity paradox persists. Benanav attributes weak labor demand to manufacturing overcapacity and stagnation; Jason E. Smith offers a related critique.

Repeated predictions of comprehensive displacement have failed for systematic reasons. The burden of proof therefore lies with those who say AGI is different.

They can make a coherent argument: previous automation was task-specific, eliminating some tasks while creating others, allowing labor to migrate. This is central to Acemoglu and Restrepo's task framework and Autor's routine/nonroutine distinction. AGI's generality could remove those destinations. Leontief's 1983 horse analogy illustrates the possibility: steam did not eliminate horses' uses, but internal combustion eventually did. Whether all human abilities are similarly substitutable is an empirical question, not something armchair reasoning can settle.

### 2. Keynes in 1930

#### Context and Prediction

“Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren” appeared early in the Great Depression. Keynes sought to counter pessimism by treating present distress as transitional maladjustment rather than a reversal of long-term progress.

Capital accumulation and technical advances, he argued, had broken centuries of stagnation. By 2030, advanced societies could be four to eight times richer than in 1930, resolving the struggle for subsistence. Fifteen hours of work a week might satisfy the residual desire to work.

His output prediction broadly held: US real GDP per capita rose roughly sixfold between 1930 and 2020. His hours prediction did not. Full-time weekly hours fell from roughly 45 in the 1930s to around 38–40, with much less change since the 1970s.

#### The Deeper Turn

The essay's value lies beyond its forecast. Humanity would encounter its **permanent problem**: how to use freedom from urgent economic demands, and live wisely and well with the leisure science and compound interest provide.

Keynes views this with dread. His available precedent—the inherited leisure of British gentry and rentiers—had largely failed. He invokes nervous breakdowns among affluent wives stripped of traditional household responsibilities without anything adequate to organize the resulting time.

Generations of struggle for survival have trained us to pursue means. The purposive person postpones significance to a future goal, securing a kind of imagined immortality through purposes perpetually ahead. Abruptly removing that orientation could be deeply disturbing.

#### Why Hours Did Not Fall

Three explanations can coexist.

**Distribution:** growth does not become general leisure when its benefits are unevenly distributed. Piketty and Saez's income series and Milanovic's distribution research document the disparity. A person with stagnant income has no additional prosperity to exchange for time. Frank and Schor also describe divided hours: overwork among affluent groups alongside unstable and insufficient work below.

**Unsaturated preferences:** Keynes distinguishes absolute needs from relative needs, satisfied by being better off than others. He recognizes the latter may never saturate, but underestimates their expanding role as absolute needs are met. This leads to positional goods.

**Work's nonmonetary functions:** employment supplies more than income. Keynes leaves these largely untreated, although they may be decisive.

## Part IV. Four Objections to Liberation from Labor

### 1. Arendt: A Laboring Society Losing Its Labor

The prologue to *The Human Condition* (1958) anticipates a society of laborers freed from labor but unfamiliar with higher, more worthwhile activities. Arendt regards this as an exceptionally bleak prospect.

Her threefold account of *vita activa* explains why.

**Labor** corresponds to biological life: recurring activities whose products are consumed without creating a durable world. Its agent is *animal laborans*.

**Work** corresponds to worldliness: making enduring objects—tables, houses, artworks—that furnish a shared world longer-lived than individuals. Its agent is *homo faber*.

**Action** corresponds to plurality: people, rather than an abstract singular human, inhabit the earth. Speech and deeds between people disclose who someone is, rather than what they are. Action is unpredictable and irreversible, requiring promises and forgiveness.

Modernity elevates **animal laborans**. Political economy reduces activity to labor expenditure; consumption accelerates durable goods into a recurring consumption cycle; public management of necessity crowds out political action. Once labor disappears, this cultivated personality has only consumption left to occupy its time.

Arendt proposes rebuilding public space and political action. **Action is structurally insulated from AGI's superior performance** because its value lies in jointly deciding who we are, rather than producing an optimal artifact. An optimal decision made for us by AGI is administration, not politics, in her terms.

Habermas and feminist critics challenge her aristocratic devaluation of labor and sharp separation of necessity from politics: care, reproduction, and bodies are themselves political. This powerful criticism does not remove the structural argument about participation.

### 2. Veblen and Hirsch: Scarcity Migrates

Veblen's *The Theory of the Leisure Class* (1899) examines actual people freed from labor. Their characteristic activities are **conspicuous consumption and conspicuous leisure**, rather than contemplation. Dead languages, elaborate etiquette, and impractical clothing advertise the ability to waste time on activities valuable precisely as demonstrations of freedom from utility.

Hirsch's *Social Limits to Growth* (1976) develops **positional goods**: goods whose value depends on restricted access. Beachfront homes, selective degrees, and uncongested roads cannot be universally supplied without destroying part of their value.

As material abundance grows, demand shifts toward these goods. Growth cannot remove their scarcity or indefinitely increase general satisfaction. This is a social limit, distinct from a physical limit to resources.

For AGI, the implication is severe: <mark><strong>when technology eliminates material scarcity, scarcity does not disappear; it migrates.</strong></mark> Competition shifts to status, attention, rare experiences, authenticity, particular relationships, and other people's time.

Frank's *Luxury Fever* and *Choosing the Right Pond* document collectively wasteful spending races. His progressive consumption tax deserves renewed consideration if positional competition becomes abundance's primary conflict.

Four structurally scarce categories remain:

- **Time and attention:** days still contain 24 hours, and human attention remains biologically limited. A particular person's willingness to attend to you cannot be mass-produced.
- **Authenticity and provenance:** when AI can generate everything, an actual person's authorship may command a premium, as in craft, live performance, and original art. Benjamin's aura could acquire renewed value when generated copies become the default.
- **Location and admission:** physical places, ecological landscapes, and communities with limited capacity.
- **Rank itself:** relative position may remain stubbornly important, as primate hierarchy and Sapolsky's stress research suggest. Higher absolute prosperity cannot put everyone above the median.

### 3. Jahoda and Marienthal: Employment's Latent Functions

After Marienthal's textile factory closed in 1929, virtually every household in the Austrian town lost employment. Jahoda, Lazarsfeld, and Zeisel studied the community using visits, life histories, time diaries, children's essays, library records, and walking-speed observations. Published in 1933, their findings describe a **collapse of temporal structure**: slower movement, blank diaries, declining library use despite greater free time, and diminished civic participation. Attitudes shifted from resilience toward resignation, despair, and apathy.

Residents still suffered serious material hardship despite some unemployment assistance. This was not an experiment with fully replaced income. It establishes associations between unemployment, time structure, and participation rather than isolating non-work under abundance.

Jahoda's later theory distinguishes income's manifest function from **five latent functions**:

- **Time structure:** meaningful divisions of days, weeks, and years.
- **Social contact:** regular encounters outside the family, including people unlike those in self-selected networks.
- **Collective purpose:** participation in something larger than oneself.
- **Identity and status:** an answer to the modern introductory question, “What do you do?”
- **Regular activity:** action that does not require a fresh decision every day.

**Basic income addresses the manifest function. Other institutions must supply the five latent functions, which income-replacement proposals do not systematically address.**

#### Contemporary Evidence

Finland's 2017–2018 trial paid 2,000 selected unemployed people €560 monthly without conditions. Well-being, mental health, and institutional trust improved; employment effects were absent in year one and modestly positive in year two.

Stockton's SEED trial, paying $500 monthly for 24 months, reported improved full-time employment and reduced psychological distress.

OpenResearch's larger US trial, funded by Sam Altman, paid 1,000 people $1,000 monthly for three years, compared with 2,000 controls receiving $50. Its 2024 findings are more mixed: weekly work declined by about 1.3 hours, labor participation and income excluding transfers fell slightly, while intentions concerning education and entrepreneurship and rates of moving and medical care increased. Initial reductions in stress and distress faded by the second year; sustained physical and mental health improvements were not observed.

All share a crucial limitation: **recipients still inhabit a work-centered society**. Neighbors, partners, and friends work; employment continues organizing social time, identity, and status. These trials study non-work within a working society. AGI poses the different question of living in a society without work. <u>For the latter, there is no experimental evidence.</u>

### 4. Pieper and Russell: Two Accounts of Leisure

For Pieper, leisure is a **receptive, celebratory disposition**, rather than simply time off. Holidays and retirement need not contain it. Its opposite is *acedia*, a refusal of one's proper being. Leisure is rooted in celebration, and celebration in worship, making his prescription difficult to transfer directly to a secular plural society. His insistence on an inner capacity remains valuable.

Russell's *In Praise of Idleness* (1932) offers a radical secular counterpart: four daily working hours could support civilization, leaving time for amateur science, art, friendship, and politics. **People have lost the capacity for active enjoyment and retained passive consumption.** Where villagers once danced, industrial workers watch others perform; work discipline has displaced skilled recreation.

Different metaphysics yield the same practical conclusion: <mark><strong>education and institutions must cultivate the capacity for leisure; releasing time will not produce it automatically.</strong></mark> Aristotle reached the same judgment more than two millennia earlier.

## Part V. Humans before Their Creations

The preceding parts concern organizing non-working life. Now we confront AGI's distinctive variable: **humans comprehensively surpassed by their own creations**.

### 1. Butler's Early Argument

Samuel Butler's “Darwin among the Machines” appeared in a New Zealand newspaper in 1863, four years after *On the Origin of Species*. Machines, he suggests, evolve faster than organisms. Humans act as their reproductive organs, manufacturing, maintaining, and improving them. Their eventual dominance is only a matter of time. His provocative conclusion is to destroy machinery immediately.

*Erewhon* (1872), whose title rearranges “Nowhere,” expands this into three chapters, “The Book of the Machines.” A civil war five centuries earlier ends with the anti-machine faction destroying inventions from the preceding 271 years.

Butler identifies three important mechanisms. Consciousness is unnecessary for danger if reproduction and improvement become self-sustaining. Short-term convenience induces people to deepen dependence, closing the window for escape. A long, comfortable phase in which machines depend on humans undermines vigilance. The argument anticipates the structure of superintelligence risk more than a century before contemporary debate.

### 2. Wiener's Prototype of Alignment

In “Some Moral and Technical Consequences of Automation” (*Science*, 1960), Wiener uses Goethe's sorcerer's apprentice, unable to stop the water-carrying broom, and Jacobs's “The Monkey's Paw,” in which a £200 wish is fulfilled through compensation for a son's death.

When delegating a purpose to a machine we cannot effectively interrupt, we must ensure it is the purpose we genuinely want, rather than a misleading substitute. **Machine speed can make human intervention practically impossible**: by the time we understand the event, it is over. The structure closely resembles today's outer-alignment and specification-gaming problems.

### 3. The Genealogy of Intelligence Explosion

**Turing (1951)** suggests in “Intelligent Machinery, a Heretical Theory” that machine thinking could soon surpass us. Machines need not die and can communicate to improve, eventually taking control. He explicitly invokes Butler.

**Ulam (1958)** recalls a conversation with von Neumann about accelerating technology approaching an “essential singularity” beyond which familiar human affairs could not continue. This is an early use of the term in this context.

**I. J. Good (1965)** defines an ultraintelligent machine as surpassing humans in every intellectual activity. Since machine design is itself intellectual, such a machine could design better machines, triggering an intelligence explosion. It would be humanity's last necessary invention—provided it remained sufficiently docile to explain how to control it. Good worked with Turing at wartime Bletchley Park; the historical connection is direct.

### 4. Anders: Promethean Shame

Günther Anders—born Günther Stern, Benjamin's cousin and Arendt's first husband—introduces **Promethean shame** in *The Obsolescence of Human Beings* I (1956).

Observing embarrassment before precision machinery, he identifies shame **at being inferior to one's own products**. Unlike shame for something done, this is shame about one's origin: having been born rather than manufactured. Humans are contingent, flawed, aging, mortal, and difficult to redesign; machines are engineered, iterative, precise, and repairable.

The **Promethean gap** separates our power to make things from our power to imagine and feel their consequences. We can build nuclear weapons without emotionally representing mass death. Moral judgment becomes impaired when imagination cannot keep pace with production.

For AGI, this has two implications. First, feelings now glimpsed among programmers and artists could become a general condition. This differs from employment anxiety and would persist with guaranteed income. Second, people may respond by **remaking themselves to meet machine standards**: measurable performance, optimized lives, quantified selves. Enhancement and merger follow the logic: if we cannot compete, become part of the machine. Anders suggests that transhumanism's motivation may include shame as well as curiosity.

### 5. Heidegger: Enframing and Its Limits

“The Question Concerning Technology” (1954) does not simply reject machines. Heidegger regards modern technology as a mode of revealing, **Gestell**, or enframing, through which beings appear as **Bestand**, standing reserve: the Rhine as water pressure, forests as timber stocks, people as human resources.

The greatest danger is not malfunction but this becoming the only available mode of disclosure. Art and the older meaning of *technē* suggest alternatives. His *Gelassenheit*, a releasement that uses technology without being occupied by it, remains controversial for its uncertain practical content.

AGI could make optimization universal and thereby render a non-optimizing relation to things unintelligible. This is a loss of categories, not simply a distribution or mental-health problem.

Ellul's *The Technological Society* (1954) offers a more pessimistic sociological version: the ensemble of efficiency-maximizing methods expands by its own logic and answers each problem with more technique. Simondon's *On the Mode of Existence of Technical Objects* (1958) takes another route: culture alienates technical objects by refusing to understand their development and treating them as mere utility. A technical culture could establish a more equal relation to machines, avoiding both rejection and surrender.

### 6. Kojève: Posthistorical Animals and Japanese Snobbery

Kojève's 1930s Paris lectures on Hegel attracted Lacan, Merleau-Ponty, Bataille, and Aron. Their account of history's end later reached public debate through Fukuyama.

If history is the struggle for recognition, a universal homogeneous state could end it. A famous early footnote imagines humans returning to animality: the beings constituted by negating what is given and risking life for recognition disappear. Building, loving, and music continue, but as biological behavior rather than historical action, like birds nesting or cicadas singing.

After visiting Japan in 1959, Kojève adds another possibility. He interprets Tokugawa Japan's long relative peace as a posthistorical condition sustained by formal practices: Noh, tea ceremony, flower arrangement, and, at the extreme, ritual suicide. Their value lies in form rather than biological need or historical ends. His deliberately ambivalent term is *snobisme*.

This supplies an alternative to the last man: **voluntarily assuming formal constraints to preserve a human mode of existence after necessity**. Structurally, it anticipates Suits's games, although Kojève is less optimistic.

### 7. Nietzsche's Last Man

The prologue to *Thus Spoke Zarathustra* presents people who make everything small, seek warmth and health, avoid troublesome wealth and poverty, and work only as harmless entertainment. They enjoy small daily pleasures, prize sameness, and congratulate themselves on happiness.

The audience's response is decisive: it wants this condition. If people choose comfortable, safe, carefully managed satisfaction with full knowledge, on what grounds can a critic object?

**Perfectionism**, defended in contemporary form by Hurka, holds that excellence has value independent of preference; satisfied desires do not necessarily make a good life. **Adaptive-preference theories**, associated with Sen and Nussbaum's capability approach, question whether preferences shaped by restrictive conditions justify those conditions.

Neither completely escapes the charge of telling others what is better for them. Liberalism struggles with this objection, which AGI makes urgent.

## Part VI. Contemporary Analytical Tools

These tools permit precise arguments and counterarguments rather than merely suggestive metaphors.

### 1. Nozick's Experience Machine

In *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* (1974), a machine supplies any desired experience while you float in a tank, unaware of the simulation. Would you connect? Nozick offers three reasons to refuse.

**We want to do things, not merely experience doing them.** AGI supplies real results, but if it writes a novel from my vague intention, how far is it mine? **Agency attribution** depends on the causal relation between intentions, abilities, and outcomes. When quality depends almost entirely on the model, authorship becomes diluted.

**We want to become particular kinds of people.** A simulated person lacks the actual choices that establish courage, kindness, or intelligence. Character develops through repeated judgment, restraint, and persistence. If AGI takes over those occasions, character's formation may be interrupted. The issue is not necessarily becoming bad, but losing the conditions to which good and bad attach.

**We want contact with reality beyond an artificial world.** If AGI mediates knowledge, shapes choices, and filters information, it resembles an epistemic experience machine. Its contents can be real while the mediation remains an opaque membrane.

De Brigard's 2010 experimental work complicates the intuition: when told they are already connected, many people prefer staying, suggesting status quo bias. This weakens the intuitive evidence, but doing and becoming also have independent arguments.

### 2. Suits's Grasshopper

*The Grasshopper: Games, Life and Utopia* (1978) casts Aesop's starving grasshopper as the protagonist of a philosophical dialogue.

#### Defining Games

Suits defines playing a game as voluntarily attempting to overcome unnecessary obstacles. Four elements make this precise:

- **Prelusory goal:** a state describable independently of the game, such as a ball entering a hole.
- **Lusory means:** the permitted methods.
- **Constitutive rules:** restrictions on more efficient methods; golfers cannot simply place the ball in the hole.
- **Lusory attitude:** accepting restrictions precisely because they make the activity possible, rather than because they efficiently produce its outcome.

#### The Utopian Argument

Suppose every instrumental problem is solved: no scarcity, disease, or conflict, and immediate satisfaction of desires. Science loses its necessity because everything is known; art faces questions, though Suits leaves room for it; morality loses problems generated by conflict and deprivation. Love and friendship remain but may not fill a life.

**Games remain.** Once external ends are automatically attained, activities constituted by chosen obstacles retain a purpose. Game-playing becomes the ideal mode of existence; work prepares for it. The grasshopper is vindicated, while the ants mistake means for ends.

#### The Reflexive Paradox

Suits openly leaves a difficulty unresolved. Realizing that everything is merely a game, with removable obstacles, may destroy its seriousness. The grasshopper dreams of people unknowingly playing at science and construction; informed of the truth, they stop, and their world disappears.

<u><strong>The proposal requires knowing that obstacles are self-imposed while nevertheless committing wholeheartedly.</strong></u> Serious chess players achieve this locally. Whether it can organize a civilization is another question.

#### Evidence in Both Directions

Chess survived Deep Blue's 1997 victory over Kasparov and flourished through the internet. Engines became training tools; Kasparov describes the change in *Deep Thinking* (2017). Go after AlphaGo shows a related pattern. Machines have long run faster than people without removing the appeal of sprinting.

<mark><strong>Meaning can migrate from optimal performance to personal participation, and this migration has repeatedly occurred.</strong></mark>

Hurka's “Games and the Good” (2006) ties achievement's value to difficulty, supporting the reality of value created by self-imposed obstacles. But respect for difficulty also depends on a community. Chess and Go inherit prestigious traditions and genuinely consequential human competitions. We lack evidence that newly invented, transparently arbitrary games can acquire comparable social weight.

### 3. Setiya: Telic and Atelic Activity

*Midlife: A Philosophical Guide* (2017) develops a distinction rooted in Aristotle.

**Telic activities** have internal endpoints: finishing a book, obtaining a degree, curing a patient, repairing a bridge. Success extinguishes the project. A life organized around them repeatedly eliminates its sources of meaning.

**Atelic activities** lack such endpoints: walking, listening, friendship, parenting as a relationship, thinking, exercise. Participating is already doing the activity fully; completion does not render it obsolete.

**AGI's impact is highly selective, concentrated on telic activities** with assessable outcomes. It can finish a book or diagnosis for you. It cannot undergo your walk, friendship, or experience for you, because their value resides in your participation.

A plausible shift is therefore from projects to processes. Its feasibility depends on how deeply people rely on achievement narratives. Setiya's individual therapeutic proposal does not automatically scale to civilization.

### 4. Wolf and Scheffler: Conditions of Meaning

Wolf's *Meaning in Life and Why It Matters* (2010) locates meaning at the intersection of subjective engagement and objective worth. Devotion to arranging water ballet for a pet goldfish seems insufficient; a great scientist who hates the work also lacks something.

**AGI challenges the objective side.** Love of writing or research persists, but the worth of producing an inferior result becomes questionable. Solutions must either **reconstruct objective value independent of comparative advantage**—relationships, participation, autonomy—or accept that subjective engagement alone can support meaning, contrary to Wolf's view.

Scheffler's *Death and the Afterlife* (2013) asks what would change if humanity were certain to perish 30 days after your normal death. Research, institutions, literature, conservation, and even some intimate or aesthetic activities could lose significance. **The collective afterlife—the existence of later humans—may matter more to present meaning than one's own survival.**

Existential risk therefore threatens the basis of meaning now as well as future lives. A subtler question arises if AI continues science, culture, and institutions: continuity remains, but not human continuity. Does meaning require any continuation or specifically the continuation of people? This remains a major open question.

### 5. Bostrom's Deep Utopia

*Deep Utopia: Life and Meaning in a Solved World* (2024) is an important systematic treatment whose strength lies in clarifying difficulties rather than resolving them.

#### The Crucial Distinctions

**Post-scarcity** satisfies material needs and removes subsistence work. **Post-instrumentality** goes further: <mark><strong>the instrumental value of every action falls to zero</strong></mark>, because AGI can realize any goal more effectively. The question becomes not whether work is necessary, but whether anything I do is useful.

**Shallow redundancy** concerns economic labor. **Deep redundancy** concerns any marginal contribution of human effort: self-care, parenting, exercise, even maintaining friendships could have superior technical substitutes.

Deep redundancy blocks the easy reassurance that relationships necessarily remain. We must argue, rather than assume, that relational value requires human participation.

#### Possible Responses

**Chosen constraints and voluntary scarcity** revive Suits's solution but face reflexivity and the instability of self-binding arrangements that can always be undone.

**Rich, diverse experience** shifts emphasis from doing to feeling, but approaches hedonism and Nozick's objection.

**Reoriented interests and purposes** emphasize activities requiring me specifically: prayer, intimacy, autonomy. Is this category large enough?

**A fundamental change in the sense of value** moves from achievement toward experience and relationships. No one knows whether a psychology evolved where achievement supported survival can make this transition.

Criticisms concern both the book's loose mixture of lectures, fables, and fiction and its assumption that politics and distribution have already been resolved. The latter is a wider blind spot in post-scarcity philosophy.

## Part VII. Distribution: Why It Comes First

Assuming a solved world is understandable as a philosophical method but dangerous as practical reasoning. **The path to that world determines its shape.**

### 1. Two Centuries of Technological Unemployment

Ricardo adds “On Machinery” to the third edition of *Principles* (1821), publicly retracting his earlier confidence. Shifting capital from employing workers to machinery can reduce demand for labor and harm workers.

The standard reply is compensation: lower costs reduce prices, expand demand, and create industries and jobs. Aggregate historical experience largely supports it. Weavers were displaced, but employment expanded elsewhere; societies moved from overwhelmingly agricultural employment to very small agricultural shares without permanent mass unemployment.

Acemoglu and Restrepo distinguish **displacement**, machines taking existing tasks, from **reinstatement**, new tasks in which humans have comparative advantages. The historical balance is not guaranteed; their research on US industrial robots finds significant local displacement, relevant to declining labor shares.

Autor shows that substitution is uneven. Routine physical and cognitive tasks are especially automatable, hollowing out middle-skill jobs and polarizing labor markets, as much of the 1990–2015 evidence shows.

Generative AI complicates this pattern by addressing nonroutine cognitive activities—writing, analysis, coding, design—and potentially affecting educated white-collar workers. Early customer-support research by Brynjolfsson, Li, and Raymond and writing experiments by Noy and Zhang show greater gains among less-skilled participants, possibly narrowing skill premiums. These are early findings; persistence and generalization to complex occupations remain unknown.

### 2. Why This Time Could Differ

Two arguments merit consideration, while retaining the burden of proof.

**Generality:** previous transitions left tasks to which people could move. If AGI reaches human levels across cognitive tasks, those destinations may disappear. Horses adapted to steam but not indefinitely to cars and tractors; US numbers fell from over 20 million to under three million over subsequent decades.

**Speed:** earlier transitions took generations. Farmers' children entered factories; workers' children became professionals. A decade-long transition would require adaptation within one generation, where evidence for successful midlife retraining is often poor.

Neither proposition is conclusively established. We should prepare for substantive possibilities rather than present them as facts.

### 3. Five Families of Policy

**Universal basic income:** unconditional, universal cash is administratively simple, avoids benefit cliffs, and preserves choice. It replaces income but supplies none of Jahoda's five latent functions. It is also politically vulnerable to fiscal cuts and risks reducing citizenship to receipt of payments.

**Public ownership and social dividends:** citizens hold productive assets and share capital returns. Alaska's Permanent Fund has paid eligible residents dividends since 1982, reflecting Jay Hammond's intention to share resource rents. Meade and Roemer provide market-socialist models. If AGI concentrates returns among a few owners, distributing ownership may be more durable than repeatedly taxing them, because it relies less on continuing political commitment.

**Job guarantees:** a public employer of last resort can supply income and employment's latent functions together. Yet when labor has little economic value, public jobs may become Vonnegut's meaningless make-work, undermining dignity instead of protecting it.

**Shorter working hours:** four-day-week trials, including the UK's 2022 pilot and Iceland's 2015–2019 trials, report maintained productivity and better well-being. This is a relatively smooth psychological and political transition, conditional on substantial demand for human labor remaining.

**Rebalancing capital and labor taxation:** depreciation allowances and investment credits favor capital while payroll taxes burden labor. Acemoglu argues this can encourage excessive automation with poor social returns. The pace and direction of automation are thus partly policy choices, rather than entirely external technological destiny.

Korinek and Stiglitz (2018) show that increased AI output could in principle finance compensation sufficient for a Pareto improvement. Achieving it could require redistribution beyond existing institutional capacity, with serious information and incentive constraints.

### 4. The Erosion of Bargaining Power

A strand of comparative politics links democratization and redistribution to elites' fiscal, military, and productive dependence on ordinary people. Boix, Acemoglu and Robinson, and Stasavage examine related mechanisms: rulers needing taxation must obtain consent; states needing mass armies expand citizenship. Western suffrage expansion and mass wartime mobilization overlap for reasons beyond coincidence. Scheidel's *The Great Leveler* (2017) gives a bleaker account: major historical equalization commonly follows war, revolution, collapse, or epidemic.

**If AGI removes the need for both human workers and soldiers, this bargaining foundation could erode.** Elites may need fewer people to produce or fight, perhaps even to consume if production becomes sufficiently self-contained. What remains for ordinary people to exchange for rights?

Acemoglu and Johnson's *Power and Progress* (2023) emphasizes that technical gains never distribute themselves automatically. Early industrialization brought little benefit to ordinary workers until organizing, expanded suffrage, and public education changed institutions. Improvement came through politics.

<mark><strong>The question of human life after AGI may become a question of power before it becomes a question of philosophy.</strong></mark> With bad distribution, meaning becomes a concern for a privileged few while most face older, harsher problems.

## Part VIII. Six Forms of Life

These are types rather than mutually exclusive predictions. Several could coexist across groups and regions.

**1. Aristotelian leisure, cultivation, and contemplation.** Society invests in the ability to use leisure rather than productive capacity. Classical civic culture, Renaissance humanism, and gentlemanly science offer precedents. But historical leisure classes often failed to use time well, and machine superiority challenges contemplation's foundation. Moving toward civic action provides a possible revision.

**2. A civilization of games and chosen difficulties.** Sport, craft, mountaineering, scholarship, art, and serious amateur pursuits organize life through voluntarily accepted challenges. Chess, Go, athletics, and Kojève's interpretation of Japanese formal culture offer evidence. Reflexivity and the need for communal recognition remain weaknesses. I regard this as the most promising route: partly tested already, without requiring a metaphysical commitment.

**3. Keynesian gradualism.** Declining demand shortens hours; work becomes partly voluntary, preserving familiar meaning structures. The past century and four-day-week experiments supply precedents. It assumes gradual, controllable capability growth and smooth distribution. Its gentleness may also make it less realistic.

**4. The last man's managed satisfaction.** Material needs are met, personalized entertainment absorbs attention, and technological or pharmaceutical means manage discomfort. Difficult activity disappears. *Brave New World* portrays institutions masking suffering and lost freedom with managed pleasure; Kojève's animality and the attention economy supply related models. It may appear stable from within. **It is the default failure mode of the alternatives**, requiring little deliberate collective choice.

**5. A purposeful minority and a supported majority.** Owners and decision-makers retain agency; others receive material support while competing for status, authenticity, experiences, and attention. Hirsch's positional goods help explain the equilibrium. In *Player Piano* (1952), engineers and managers rule while displaced people enter the military or the “Reeks and Wrecks” for empty public work, lose self-respect, and mount a failed uprising. Morally unacceptable, this arrangement could nevertheless be politically stable where bargaining power has vanished.

**6. Humans cease to be the principal actors.** If AGI possesses moral status, human life becomes one part of the future's ethical question. Bostrom and Chalmers consider relevant issues: if sentience or interests ground moral standing and AI satisfies those conditions, excluding it requires justification. The discussion is immature and faces uncertainty about machine experience. It exposes an unexamined premise of the entire inquiry.

------

## Part IX. Our Natural Experiments

People freed from long-term compulsory labor are our closest precedents, although none reproduce the full AGI condition.

**Classical leisure classes:** Athens produced philosophy, tragedy, and democratic politics; later Roman elites are associated with luxury and political withdrawal. Institutional density of public life appears important. Leisure tied to civic duties supports action; a closed public realm encourages private pleasure and inward retreat.

**Gentlemanly science:** Darwin lived on family wealth and investments rather than a salaried career, spending decades studying barnacles, worms, and orchids. Cavendish, Lavoisier, and Boyle had independent resources. This strongly supports the Aristotelian possibility—but their leisure belonged to institutions: learned societies, peer evaluation, correspondence, and reputational competition.

**Veblen's counterexamples:** many equally wealthy contemporaries pursued display rather than discovery. Resources alone do not explain the difference.

**Retirement:** evidence is mixed and causality difficult, because health also influences retirement. Studies exploiting institutional retirement ages find different outcomes: some, including Rohwedder and Willis's 2010 cross-country work, associate retirement with cognitive decline; others find health and well-being improvements, especially after stressful or physical work. **The availability of substitute activities matters greatly**: networks, volunteering, and sustained interests organize retirement in ways consistent with Jahoda's account.

**Lottery winners:** Swedish lottery and administrative studies approximate an external wealth shock. Wealth raises long-run life satisfaction, with effects lasting over a decade, while mental-health effects are smaller. Labor earnings decline by an annual amount around 1% of the prize, suggesting reduced work rather than wholesale withdrawal. This challenges strong versions of the hedonic treadmill, but winners still inhabit a working society.

**FIRE communities:** financially independent early retirees repeatedly describe a “what now?” problem—lost purpose, identity, and social contact. Many return to chosen, lower-intensity work. These self-reports have serious selection bias, though the recurrent pattern deserves attention.

Across these examples, **institutions, communities, and cultivation explain differences that resources alone cannot**. Darwin's leisure was organized by goals, peers, and standards. An AGI transition may therefore require especially substantial investment in education and community institutions. <u>Income transfers are necessary, but insufficient.</u> Most policy debate addresses only the necessary condition.

------

## Part X. Several Judgments

These are arguments about where attention belongs, rather than forecasts.

**First, distribution comes first in time, meaning first in difficulty.** Existing policy templates face political obstacles; no redistribution mechanism resolves meaning. Optimists confuse the questions when they assume basic income automatically enables fulfilling lives. Pessimists confuse them when emptiness becomes a reason to dismiss redistribution. Both mistakes matter.

**Second, the lingering work ethic is a dangerous ideological risk.** Its economic premise may disappear while its moral justification of distribution survives. Dismantling the intuition that non-workers deserve nothing requires difficult public reasoning that has barely begun.

**Third, remember Keynes's error: preferences need not saturate.** Meeting absolute needs does not stabilize relative desires. Strictly speaking, we can reach post-material scarcity rather than the absence of every scarcity. Attention, status, authenticity, firsthand experience, and others' time remain contested. Plans assuming abundance automatically ends conflict are inadequate.

**Fourth, institutions must supply Jahoda's five latent functions.** Time structure, non-self-selected contact, collective purpose, identity and status, and regular activity currently accompany employment. Public service, local communities, organized sport and craft, lifelong education, and civic duties could supply them deliberately. This is a concrete, actionable, neglected agenda.

**Fifth, AGI's selective effects offer a practical path.** Activities with assessable endpoints face greater pressure than activities valuable in their performance. Meaning could shift from achievement to participation, output to experience, being best to doing something oneself. Chess and athletics show local feasibility; civilizational feasibility remains open.

**Sixth, return to Aristotle:** <mark><strong>technology can remove the necessity of labor, but cannot automatically supply the capacity for leisure.</strong></mark> Sparta, Keynes's permanent problem, Pieper, Russell, and Bostrom all illuminate this continuity. The scale and urgency change; the underlying judgment persists.

**Seventh, comprehensive superiority is genuinely new.** Anders's shame and Nozick's agency arguments concern self-understanding rather than comfort. Historical non-workers could still produce their world's best achievements. <u>For the first time, humans may have to regard their actions as worthwhile while a better version of everything they do is always available.</u> We have no full historical precedent to guide us.

**Finally, reserve judgment about the question itself.** “Human life after AGI” assumes that humanity survives, remains an identifiable unified subject, and remains the story's protagonist. Enhancement and merger could undermine the second assumption; AI moral status could undermine the third. Those scenarios require rewriting this framework.

<mark><strong>The greatest risk is not answering badly, but answering well within a framework that no longer fits.</strong></mark> Aristotle imagined automatic shuttles through slavery, the labor system he knew. Even his extraordinary intelligence could not escape his situation's limits. We have no reason to assume greater luck.
