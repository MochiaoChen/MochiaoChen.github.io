> **This is a textbook case of having a hammer and going in search of a nail.**
>
> When the numbers conflict with the preferred narrative, the narrative survives—and the most obvious denominator quietly disappears.

An article in *Sanlian Lifeweek* discussed a fact that deserves serious attention: between 2002 and 2022, male undergraduate entrants rose from roughly 918,000 to around 1.76 million, while female entrants climbed from about 669,000 to 2.94 million. Yet the argument returned to a familiar story of female victimhood, and its policy sympathy continued to flow in only one direction.

The question is not whether girls have faced injustice historically, or whether women still face it in other domains. They plainly do. The question is narrower: **when we are talking about access to undergraduate education today, can we acknowledge that the direction of disadvantage at this particular stage has changed?**

<mark>If an equality narrative can recognise female failure but not male failure, it is no longer a general theory of equality. It is a moral drama with a permanently assigned protagonist.</mark>

## Begin with elementary arithmetic

China recorded approximately **15.93 million births** in 2004. Contemporary demographic sources put the sex ratio at birth at roughly **121.2 boys for every 100 girls**.[^birth-en]

That gives a rough split:

$$
\text{Boys}\approx15.93\times\frac{121.2}{221.2}=8.73\text{ million}
$$

$$
\text{Girls}\approx15.93\times\frac{100}{221.2}=7.20\text{ million}
$$

The Ministry of Education reports about **4.679 million** undergraduate entrants in regular and vocational higher-education institutions in 2022. Around **2.948 million were women**, leaving approximately **1.731 million men**.[^moe2022-en]

| Rough comparison: 2004 births and 2022 undergraduate entrants | Male | Female |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Estimated births | 8.73m | 7.20m |
| Undergraduate entrants in 2022 | 1.731m | 2.948m |
| Approximate ratio | **19.8%** | **40.9%** |

In this deliberately rough comparison:

> **Out of every 100 girls, about 41 appear in the undergraduate intake. Out of every 100 boys, about 20 do. The female figure is 2.06 times the male figure.**

Women were not merely a slim majority. They represented approximately **63%** of undergraduate entrants. In a pathway that strongly shapes occupations, earnings expectations, and social networks, that is a structural gap—not random noise.

### What this calculation cannot prove

This is not a strict cohort enrolment rate. Not everyone entering university in 2022 was born in 2004. Grade repetition, early entry, mortality, migration, and differences in statistical definitions all affect the comparison. A genuine sex-disaggregated cohort rate would require linked year-by-year population and student records.

Those limitations matter. They do not, however, plausibly explain a gap of more than twenty percentage points and roughly two to one. The calculation is useful not because it delivers a perfect rate, but because it asks the question the media account should not have avoided: **in a cohort that began with more boys than girls, who was lost more rapidly as it moved through the education pipeline?**

## Can the winner keep permanent ownership of victimhood?

The troubling feature of the article is not that it cares about women. It is that it appears capable of caring only about women.

Girls may still face genuine disadvantages in some rural households, labour markets, pay, and unpaid care. Acknowledging female advantage in undergraduate entry erases none of that. But **disadvantage in domain A cannot justify ignoring advantage in domain B, nor can it serve as an automatic claim on one-directional policy support inside domain B.**

Educational equality must answer to the current distribution of opportunity, not to slogans preserved from an earlier distribution.

When women hold roughly 63% of undergraduate places, but the camera still turns instinctively to the tears of women at the top of the pyramid—and policy proposals still ask how to direct more support toward girls—the implied message is clear: male failure requires no social explanation. It is merely personal failure.

That is a peculiar narrative privilege: **a substantial numerical advantage in admission combined with permanent ownership of the moral position of the underdog.**

## Where did the other eighty boys go?

This question matters more than “why are there more women on campus?”

China’s 2022 monitoring report on women’s development shows that girls made up **42.1%** of students in secondary vocational education, meaning boys were **57.9%**. Women were **46.7%** of students in higher vocational programmes, leaving men at **53.3%**.[^women2022-en]

These are enrolment stocks, not flows. They cannot be matched one-for-one with a single year’s undergraduate admissions, nor can they tell us the destination of each boy who did not enter a bachelor’s programme. But the direction is consistent: **the closer the pathway moves toward academic undergraduate education, the larger the female advantage; male representation rises again in vocational tracks.**

Some boys move into vocational schools and colleges. Others leave full-time education earlier for factories, construction sites, logistics, delivery work, maintenance, and lower-tier services. Existing aggregate data cannot tell us exactly where each hundred boys ends up. “The data are incomplete,” however, is not the same as “the problem does not exist.”

<mark>People filtered out of university do not disappear from society. They disappear only from the spaces that journalists know best and find easiest to write about.</mark>

## A provocation: basic education systematically penalises boys

> **A provocation—and a mechanism that still requires more evidence: in several of its core evaluative practices, modern basic education exerts systematic pressure against boys.**

“Systematic” does not mean teachers are conspiring against boys. It does not mean girls’ results are unearned. It means that an apparently neutral institution repeatedly rewards developmental rhythms and behavioural traits that are not distributed identically across the sexes, while offering too few equivalent routes to success for students with different profiles.

Modern schooling is highly standardised. It asks students to:

- remain seated and attentive for long periods;
- follow a common pace and disciplinary order;
- complete large volumes of repetitive work on time;
- minimise mistakes under continuous assessment;
- allow organisation, neatness, and behavioural compliance to enter the academic score.

These capacities have value. But when they are compressed into a single route to success, **children who mature later, need more movement, or have weaker impulse control pay a higher price—and those traits are, on average, more common among boys.**

UNESCO now treats boys’ disengagement as a global education issue. In many settings, boys are at greater risk of repeating grades, failing to progress or complete school, and remaining enrolled without achieving adequate learning.[^unesco-en]

This is not merely an invention of online gender conflict. It is a problem that international education institutions increasingly address directly.

## Later brain development: a clue, not a verdict

A classic longitudinal MRI study found that total brain-volume trajectories in its sample peaked at approximately **10.5 years for females** and **14.5 years for males**. Turning points in several cortical grey-matter regions also appeared earlier, on average, among females.[^lenroot-en]

This offers a biological clue for the claim that boys mature later on average. Three qualifications are essential:

1. **Peak brain volume is not peak intelligence, nor is it a direct measure of classroom ability.**
2. These are group averages with substantial overlap between individual boys and girls.
3. Structural imaging does not directly establish causation for school outcomes; family, culture, nutrition, puberty, teacher interaction, and peer norms all intervene.

Neuroscience therefore does not prove that schools discriminate against boys. It supports a narrower and more useful conclusion: **treating equal chronological age as equal developmental readiness may itself be a faulty assumption.**

If two groups with different average developmental timelines compete at the same age, in the same classroom, under the same continuous discipline-based assessment—and the result is explained entirely as “effort”—the system may be less neutral than it appears.

## The reversal at doctoral level complicates the story

In a 2018 response to the National People’s Congress, the Ministry of Education reported that women made up **53.44%** of undergraduate students and **53.14%** of master’s students in 2016, but only **38.63%** of doctoral students.[^moe2016-en]

This cross-section does not prove that penalties against boys fade only after graduate school. Doctoral participation also reflects field composition, willingness to continue, opportunity costs, marriage and childbearing pressures, and labour-market choices. What it does show is that **gender advantage is not a straight line extending unchanged from primary school to the highest degree.**

That is precisely why one-dimensional stories—whether “women are always oppressed” or “men are always oppressed”—are inadequate. Fair policy must be disaggregated by age, educational stage, location, household background, and field of study. No group should carry a victim’s licence that remains valid in every context forever.

## The answer is not simply to subsidise the other sex

Identifying a crisis among boys does not require abandoning girls. Nor should scarce support simply be redirected into a new “boys only” programme. That would reproduce the same intellectual laziness.

A more credible agenda would begin with:

1. **Publishing a complete sex-disaggregated education pipeline**, from primary school through tracking, exams, undergraduate and vocational education, dropout, and employment.
2. **Treating reading difficulty, disciplinary sanctions, grade repetition, and disengagement as early-warning indicators for boys**, rather than discovering the problem after university admission.
3. **Reducing dependence on a single evaluative channel**, with more project work, oral explanation, practical work, and repeated opportunities to demonstrate learning.
4. **Strengthening progression and labour-market returns in vocational education**, so it does not become a holding area for students labelled as failures.
5. **Targeting actual disadvantage**, combining sex with rurality, income, household structure, disability, and regional inequality.

OECD research similarly shows that reading gaps, disciplinary climate, teacher relationships, and assessment can affect boys and girls differently. The answer is not a stereotyped “boy pedagogy”, but a school system more responsive to meaningful differences among students.[^oecd-en]

## The overlooked child also has a sex

The *Sanlian* article saw the women inside the university. It did not truly see the men outside it.

The boys missing from campus have not vanished. They are in vocational schools, factories, streets, fields, residential compounds, and delivery depots still lit after midnight. They are less able to translate failure into the language favoured by media institutions, and they are rarely packaged as a public story deserving sympathy.

This essay does not argue that girls should not win. It rejects a quieter double standard: **female disadvantage is explained structurally, while male disadvantage is explained individually; female success is a breakthrough, while male failure is treated as deserved.**

<mark>Equality does not mean speaking forever for the same side. It means being willing to turn toward whoever is falling behind now.</mark>
